Piazza Bellini was already filling out by the time the umbrella over Andrew's table threw a clean circle of shade across the page. The morning around him had its own pace. Scooters threaded the far edge of the square. Two women debated a parking space without much heat in it. A pigeon worked the base of the statue for crumbs Andrew could not see. Roberto already knew the table — he had ordered without ordering.

The book was a novel he had found in a secondhand stall on his first week in Naples, and had been carrying from café to café without urgency. Months in the city now. Long enough to have a place that knew his table; long enough to have spent more nights as Super Orso on the rooftops above this square than days at a table beneath them. The daylight version of the city sat in him differently than the night one did.

He read three sentences. A wind moved across the piazza, light enough that he might have missed it had it not found his open collar. It travelled into his chest hair. He felt it there. Physical, small. A fact of his body he had not had a year ago.

Roberto came through with the espresso and a glass of water on a small tray. He set them down with the steady economy of a man who had done this several thousand times.

"_Ehi, amico mio._"

"_Buongiorno, Roberto._"

Roberto's glance had landed on the back of the café before it found him. A small thing. Something about a delivery, a kitchen issue, a shift change. Andrew filed it as nothing, drank his water, and went back to the page.

Andrew lifted the espresso. His sleeve pulled back along his forearm and the inside of his upper arm came into view — heavy with muscle, dark with hair. He held on the sight half a second before drinking. The cup went back to its saucer with a small clean sound.

The square continued. A woman with two small dogs came past and the dogs looked up at his shoes before deciding he was not interesting. The page pulled him back under.

---

The delivery van pulled up at the corner where deliveries always pulled up.

Andrew tracked it without lifting his head. An old habit, sharpened by the new work. The van was unfamiliar. He filed the unfamiliarity and kept reading.

The man who got out was wrong.

It registered before any single detail had organised itself into a reason. He was too thick through the chest for a delivery driver. The branded shirt sat on him with the slight tension of something rented for the role. His boots were too good for the rest of the costume. The stride from van to café held a steadiness real delivery drivers did not bother with. He carried two stacked boxes without making the small adjustments a man carrying real boxes makes.

He passed Andrew's table. Andrew did not lift his eyes to the face. His body had registered something before any detail had finished arriving — a small uneven note from somewhere lower than thought. He had carried the same energy once. Different intensity. Same key. He knew the frequency without being able to name it.

Roberto came past Andrew's table and did not stop to refill the water. Andrew had not asked him to. Roberto's body had the contracted stillness of a man who had been told to expect this and told not to react.

Andrew held the page open and read nothing.

He had been coming to this café for months.

The thought arrived before he could decide whether he wanted it. Roberto's nod, the umbrella over the marble, the espresso that came without being asked for, the same chair every morning — all of it sitting above whatever was being moved through the back. He had been within twenty feet of it every time he ordered.

He set the cup down. Closed the book on his finger.

The delivery man was inside the back room now. Andrew counted to ten. Then he stood, slid a note under the saucer, and walked off the piazza onto the via. The deep doorway of a palazzo he had clocked weeks ago for unrelated reasons.

He stepped into it.

The transformation took less than ten seconds. He had done it enough now that the process closed itself around him without instruction. Heat surged from his core upward and outward, mass settled along his limbs in a single sustained movement, and the suit arrived already on him — blue across the chest, white piping clean from collar to boot, the cape weighting his shoulders.

He stepped out of the doorway as Super Orso and went back to the café at a pace urgent enough to read as serious without reading as panic.

The front of the café had emptied. A woman at the bar had taken one look at him through the open door and left her cappuccino. Roberto was at the till, white in the face. Andrew gave him nothing on the way past.

The back room was the size of a small garage. Crates stacked against three of the walls. A single bulb hanging on a cord. Three men.

Two were on their feet before they had registered who he was. The third — the driver — was crouched over a crate with one hand inside it and turned without standing. His eyes went violet in the low light.

Andrew read the room before he had fully crossed the threshold. The two on their feet were thicker than the driver, similar build to each other, the same flat black at the wrist. The space had no good angles for constructs that wanted distance. Crates limited the width. The bulb limited the light. A door at the back was half-blocked by stacked cardboard with no clear path to it.

The first man came at him.

Andrew's hand opened and a small blue shield flickered into shape, the size of a pizza tray. He turned the punch with it and drove his elbow into the man's neck. Real strength behind the punch — more than a man of that size should have had. Andrew filed the recalibration and kept moving. The man fell back into shelving that held him without breaking, when by every right it should have broken.

The second came in fast. Andrew underestimated him by the exact margin he had failed to update for, and the hit caught him across the jaw hard enough that he tasted blood. He went sideways into a crate and the crate went over and the contents — small dark bottles in foam — clinked against each other. He kept his feet.

His estimate updated.

He gathered another construct, larger this time, a curved scutum the length of his forearm, and used it as a wedge. Drove the second man off-balance with the long edge and pinned him against the back wall by weight rather than force. The driver had risen and was coming for his unprotected side with something heavy in his hand. Andrew rotated the shield's angle and caught the descending arm at the rim and broke the swing before it landed. Took a second hit to the ribs before he could close — a hard one, the kind that would matter later.

He brought the rim down across the driver's collarbone hard enough that the driver's body decided to stop. The driver dropped. The first man was getting up off the floor. Andrew stepped onto his chest with controlled weight and the man's breath went out of him.

The second, pinned, swung wildly. The swing went into shelving rather than into Andrew. He freed one hand, formed a smaller construct — a length of geometric rope, blue and bright as glass — and looped the man's wrist to the steel upright. The man fought it. The rope held.

He bound the driver next, then the first man, who tried once more to rise and then stopped trying.

Three of them. Ninety seconds, maybe. Andrew was breathing hard. His ribs would hurt for days. There was blood at the corner of his mouth.

He stood for ten seconds without moving.

Then he turned and looked at what he had stepped into.

The crates against the wall were not all carrying the same thing. Half held the heavy bottles he had heard clink earlier — small, in dark glass, sealed with a crimped metal cap. Another set held folded leather harnesses with thick straps and heavy buckles. The strap gauge was the same as the one he had not handled since a warehouse on the other side of the world. Wristbands beside them, the rubber black against the leather.

Andrew did not lift the harness. His hand still recognised the dimensions without needing the contact.

The bottles were what stopped him longer.

He picked one up. Heavy for its size, the weight not matching its volume. The glass dark enough that the contents read as black. He held it in his palm and felt it — a small pull, low and steady, his body answering before his mind had decided whether to. Specific. Not toward anything good.

He had carried this once. Through his lungs, that time, rather than in glass. Something in the bottle was a version of what Bill had pushed through him with the cigar.

He weighed the recklessness of taking it against the recklessness of leaving it. He took it. Wrapped it in a clean rag from a shelf and slipped it into the inner pocket of his jacket. The pocket sat against his ribs. He felt the weight of it there. More than weight.

He stepped back through the front of the café.

---

Roberto was where he had been left, white-faced, both hands flat against the bar. He looked at Super Orso and looked away.

Andrew stepped out and turned back onto the via. He came out of the palazzo doorway as Andrew. The bottle's weight against his ribs did not tell on him through the jacket.

He pushed through the café door.

Roberto looked up. Could not hold the look. His hand kept working a cloth across the bar in a motion not connected to cleaning anything.

"Sit," Andrew said, in Italian.

Roberto did not sit. His mouth opened and closed twice. The third time he spoke.

"My nephew works the deliveries." His voice flat. "Eighteen. They came to my sister's apartment in February. Three of them. They explained him in detail. Then they explained the café."

Andrew did not respond. There was no shape to a response that would hold.

"I never saw what they brought." Roberto's hand stopped. "I never asked. I thought, if I never asked—" He stopped again. Tried again. "Last week, two of them were here, arguing about a shipment. One said the next one was for Marseille. The other corrected him. He said three of the bottles, only three, were for Marseille. The others were going further."

"How much further."

"He didn't say. He said it like a man whose job included countries I would not have guessed at." Roberto's hand started moving again, found nothing useful, stopped. "I'm sorry. I should not have let you—"

"Don't apologise to me." Andrew kept his voice flat because he had nothing softer that would hold. "Lock the back. Call your nephew before they do. Then call the police about a robbery in the back room, and tell them only that. I will not have been here."

Roberto nodded. Nodded again.

Andrew stepped out onto the piazza.

---

The afternoon had arrived without his noticing the change. The piazza was bright, people were eating, Naples was being itself in sunshine, indifferent to the weight in his pocket. He started walking back toward the apartment without choosing the route.

The bottle pulled at him. Quietly, persistently, toward nothing in particular — toward itself. Whatever was in it had recognised whatever was still in him. He kept walking and did not look at the pocket.

His phone was in the other pocket. He had forgotten he was carrying it. He took it out for something to do with his hand, and the screen woke up, and there was a notification waiting for him that he had not heard arrive.

A message from Mike.

He had not heard from Mike in six weeks. The last thing in their thread was a selfie from somewhere mid-air — both of them in their suits, Nick laughing into the camera. Andrew had sent back emojis.

He opened the new message.

_I need your help. It's Nick._

He stopped walking. He stood at the edge of a small square where the via opened out, people moving past him on both sides, and read the six words again.

The afternoon kept moving around him. He did not.
