# Bernham — Location Reference
 
## Overview
 
An English-speaking coastal port city with a real skyline. Deliberately unplaced — plausibly North American or North/West European, never stated.
 
The old brick core — dense, narrow-streeted, layered, built in the late 19th century — is still standing. It hasn't been cleared to make room for what came after; the new construction rose over and around it instead. Modern office towers and tall apartment buildings push up behind the historic centre. Glass and steel over an older grid. The docklands sit flat at the waterfront, old industrial infrastructure mixed with converted warehouse spaces and pockets of public access. Inland the city climbs, residential neighbourhoods rising above the harbour.
 
The atmosphere is bright. Coastal light, open, alive. There is grit — in the docklands, in the older residential streets, in the parts of the city that didn't get the glass treatment — but it's a texture rather than a tone. Bernham is not a moody city. It has energy and density and the slightly unsettled feeling of a place that is still becoming something.
 
The Medved Bridge spans the harbour entrance — a suspension bridge, cable-draped and elegant, its towers visible against the sky from most of the city. Named for Viktor Medved, a founding figure of Bernham's early history and the bridge's namesake in later civic tribute. It is functional — traffic crosses it, the port moves around it — and it is the thing people picture when they think of Bernham.
 
To the east, a mountain range sits at mid-distance — clearly present, visible from most of the city, framing the skyline without crowding it. Bernham sits between two geographies: the open harbour to the west, the mountains to the east.
 
Mike blended into it before his transformation. He operates above it now. The Brotherhood has been working inside it for years without anyone credible enough to notice.
 
---
 
## Geography and Scale
 
**Population:** Undecided. The city has a skyline and a working port — it reads as a substantial urban centre, not a large town. Exact scale to be confirmed.
 
**Waterfront:** The city grew around a natural harbour on its western edge. Flat at the water's edge — docklands, warehouses, the harbour itself — then rising eastward inland. The Medved Bridge spans the harbour entrance, its cables and towers forming the dominant element of the waterfront skyline. Afternoon and evening light catches the water; the bridge silhouettes against the western sky at sunset.
 
**Topography:** Flat to the west at the waterfront, climbing eastward toward the mountain range. The docklands and the historic core sit at low elevation on the western side. Residential neighbourhoods occupy the rise to the east. The underground infrastructure benefits from this: the oldest construction is at the lowest point, which is also the deepest.
 
**Surroundings:** To the east, the city gives way to suburbs and then to the foothills of the mountain range — clearly visible from the city, mid-distance, not looming. To the northwest, the terrain is different: suburbs softening into mixed woodland. Daddy Bear's cabin is in this zone — accessible from the city but in genuine woodland, away from the mountain terrain to the east.

## Districts
 
### The Docklands
 
The old working waterfront. A mix of states: active industrial infrastructure alongside converted warehouses, some pockets of public access — a stretch of open quayside, a café in a repurposed transit shed — sitting next to sections that are still chain-link and concrete and working machinery. The Medved Bridge is visible from the docklands, its towers rising at the harbour entrance.
 
The Black Ursa concert in Chapter 5 takes place in a warehouse here: wide, low-ceilinged, concrete-floored, still carrying its industrial past in the acoustics and the smell. That section is in the less-converted end — somewhere between working port and abandoned, the kind of space that hosts events because nobody has decided what else to do with it yet.
 
The mixed character is the point. The Brotherhood used the ambiguity of the unconverted sections for early recruitment operations. In the converted areas, unfamiliar faces look like visitors. In the industrial sections, nobody is counting.
 
**Canonical:** Chapter 5 concert venue — "warehouse at the edge of the old dockyards"
 
---
 
### The Old City Centre
 
The original commercial heart of Bernham — the late 19th century brick grid that the rest of the city grew around. Dense and narrow-streeted, ground floor retail and hospitality beneath upper-floor offices and flats, the kind of urban fabric that accumulated slowly and was never comprehensively cleared. Tanner Row runs through this district. Kuma Coffee sits on its western edge where it meets Orson Park and Harbour End.
 
This is where Mike walks each morning — through streets that are older than anything else in the city, in the direction of the towers.
 
---
 
### Downtown
 
The high-rise core. A substantial cluster of office towers and tall residential apartment buildings rising above the old city grid to the east of the historic centre. Glass and steel, significant height, the kind of skyline density that reads unmistakably from the harbour, from the Medved Bridge, and from the air.
 
This is the part of Bernham that is still becoming something. New towers have gone up within the last decade. Others are going up now. The skyline is not finished.
 
At street level, the downtown is loud with working city — commuters, deliveries, coffee queues, construction hoardings around the bases of buildings that haven't opened yet. Higher up, it is glass and reflection and the mountain range framed in office windows to the east.
 
For Superbear, the downtown is the most navigable part of the city from the air — towers close enough together to move between, high enough to clear, the harbour visible to the west and the mountains to the east as fixed bearings.
 
**Canonical:** Implied throughout Ch1 and the series by "large modern city" descriptor; skyline referenced in Overview

### The King Street District
 
Residential, one remove from the centre. Older housing stock, some tree-lined streets, the kind of neighbourhood that went from working-class density to mixed-use without anything dramatic happening to it either way. Affordable in the way that places become affordable when they're not interesting enough to gentrify completely.
 
King Street is long. The park is the notable feature on it — no need for a street number in the text. The park has a central fountain, a main lamp-lit path, lamp posts spaced widely enough to leave genuine dark between them, trees close to the verge past the fountain. Large enough that someone inside it after dark is not visible from the perimeter.
 
This is where Nick operates in Chapter 14 — likely for surveillance rather than because he lives here. Its relationship to Harbour End and Orson Park within Bernham's geography is unresolved.
 
**Canonical:** Park on King, fountain, lamp-lit paths, bench, trees (Ch14)

### Delmar Street / East Residential
 
Residential. Multi-storey housing, apartment buildings. The fire in Chapter 3 is in a building here — the news description implies height (upper floors visible in the live feed) and multiple occupants. The street is unremarkable enough to need no further specification and accessible enough for a residential emergency to make local news.
 
The neighbourhood reads as denser and slightly older than the King Street district. Closer to the city centre or south-east of it.
 
**Canonical:** Residential building fire on Delmar Street (Ch3, news broadcast)

### Orson Park
 
Mike's neighbourhood. Mixed transitional character — late 19th century brick buildings alongside newer infill construction, neither gentrified nor derelict. The kind of place that has been meaning to become something for decades without committing to a direction. Ground floor shops with flats above, some newer builds slotted into gaps between older terraces, a park that gives the neighbourhood its name.
 
It is close enough to the Central District for Mike to walk to work. His apartment is here — a few floors up, window facing the street. He knows the sidewalk outside by feel.
 
**Etymology note:** Orson derives from French *ourson* (bear cub), from *ours* (bear). Consistent with Bernham's hidden bear-reference pattern. Whether this is the neighbourhood's in-universe origin is unresolved.
 
**Canonical:** Mike's apartment (Ch1, Ch2), Mike's morning routine
 
---
 
### Harbour End
 
Nick's neighbourhood. Docklands-adjacent, slightly rough, more transient than Orson Park. The population turns over — people arrive for work, for port-adjacent industry, for cheap rent — without the neighbourhood ever quite cohering into a community. There are streets that feel different after dark.
 
Nick's small apartment is here, affordable on an insurance clerk's salary. He has lived here long enough to know which routes to take and which to avoid, without it ever feeling threatening — just textured. He doesn't think of it as rough. He thinks of it as his.
 
The proximity to the docklands means Nick was living close to the Brotherhood's operational territory before he had any reason to know they existed.
 
**Canonical:** Nick's apartment (Nick character sheet — "small apartment in a large city")

### Kuma Coffee
 
Sits on the border between Orson Park and Harbour End — the natural threshold between the two neighbourhoods. Both Mike and Nick are regulars. Nick has been coming for weeks. Mike has been coming long enough for Nick to have noticed him without registering why.
 
An independent café, better than its location suggests. The counter is wooden and apparently not rated for superhuman strength.
 
Kuma Coffee is not related to Power Bear Coffee. It is an independent café that occupies the transitional zone between their two lives.
 
**Etymology note:** *Kuma* (クマ) is Japanese for bear — consistent with Bernham's hidden bear-reference pattern across multiple languages.
 
**Canonical:** Counter (dented by Mike), condiment bar, window table, TV screen showing news, mid-morning ambient noise (Ch3)
 
**Open:** Street address unresolved.

### The Underground
 
Not a named district. Not mapped. Not visible.
 
The Brotherhood's lair occupies a deep section of the city's oldest sub-street infrastructure — stone and brick construction from the port's operational peak, sealed and overlaid with twentieth-century concrete when the surface above was redeveloped and the original use became obsolete.
 
**What this infrastructure is:** Old bonded storage vaults or commercial cellars from the docklands' active period. Purpose-built for holding goods in transit — dry, deep, sealed against the weather, designed to be moved through in bulk. When the port contracted and the surface above was redeveloped, the vaults were sealed at the access points rather than demolished. They have been under the city ever since, structurally sound and increasingly forgotten.
 
The Brotherhood found them. Cleared the access. Have been there long enough to make it function.
 
**Why it shields Nick:** Not a deliberate countermeasure. Geography. Stone, brick, and concrete at depth, with the interference of a dense working city overhead. Mike's tracking capability loses the trail before it reaches the surface above the lair. The underground is not hidden. It is simply deep.
 
**Narrative note:** The arc will need one low-key in-universe anchor for why nobody stumbled on this during subsequent development — a single line or implied detail is enough. The most plausible explanation is that the access was sealed from above, the vaults are below a section of the docklands that was built over rather than dug up, and the Brotherhood's access point is through a section of the old docklands they control at surface level. This does not need to be stated explicitly. It needs to be consistent.

## The University District
 
Home to the **University of Bernham**, which includes **Medved College** as one of its named constituent colleges. The university has a visible campus district — student housing, a campus gym, and enough concentrated foot traffic from international and domestic students to support its own economy. Andrew was here as an Italian exchange student before his corruption.
 
The district is urban rather than enclosed — buildings are distributed through a city neighbourhood rather than set behind gates. It has notice boards, a gym, the social infrastructure of a campus. It sits close enough to the central district that a student could walk between them.
 
The Brotherhood recruited Andrew here. The campus ad was placed precisely where a broke, isolated exchange student would see it — on or near campus, in the kind of shared space where students look for work, housing, or opportunities. Bill did not find Andrew by accident. Whether the ad was placed specifically within Medved College is unresolved — and worth holding as a potential detail for when Andrew's backstory is examined in the main arc.
 
**Canonical:** "university district" (Andrew character sheet), campus gym, campus notice board / Brotherhood ad placement (Remaking Andrew)
 
**Locked:** University of Bernham; Medved College (constituent college, named for Viktor Medved)

## Tanner Row and the Queer and Bear Community
 
**Tanner Row** is Bernham's equivalent of Canal Street — a strip in the old city centre, named for the leather trade that once operated there, now the established heart of the city's LGBTQ+ scene. The name carries no inherent queer coding, which is part of why it works. It was a trade street before it was a community, and the community claimed it without the street being redesigned for them.
 
The city has an established LGBTQ+ scene — not a Pride capital, not nationally visible, but a real community with long-running venues, distinct subcultures, and a visible bear and leather component. Black Ursa plays to that crowd.
 
**The Paw & Anchor** is the established bear bar. Anthony knows its name before he ever sets foot inside it — he has followed it on social media, scrolled past its photos, admired it from a distance the way someone treats a place they've decided isn't for them. Interior: warm wood panelling, amber overhead lighting, bar with a mirror behind it, dartboard area, tables. It is loud, warm, and welcoming in a way that functions as the story's first image of what consensual bear community looks like from the outside.
 
The Paw & Anchor sits on or just off Tanner Row — "just down the block" from the street where Mike intervened with Anthony. It is not hidden, not underground, not a secret. It is simply a place that most people don't know about because they haven't needed to.
 
This is exactly what Bill is targeting. The community is large enough that his conversion operations don't require external travel, dense enough that event venues can draw self-selected crowds, and small enough that his growth has gone unnoticed by anyone with the standing or awareness to act on it. He has been here long enough to be understood as part of the scene rather than a recent arrival. That's deliberate.
 
Mike and Nick are embedded in this community but not central to it. Mike's transformation isolated him from ordinary queer social life in ways neither of them has fully examined. Nick's engagement with the community since then has been largely investigative.
 
**The Grizzly Den** and **Thick Thursdays at Murphy's** are also mentioned by name in Anthony's story as places he has followed online. These are either additional venues in the same district or events at existing venues. They do not need to be placed precisely until a chapter requires them.
 
**Canonical:** The Paw & Anchor — name, street proximity, full interior description (Antonio story). The Grizzly Den, Murphy's — names only, locations unspecified.
