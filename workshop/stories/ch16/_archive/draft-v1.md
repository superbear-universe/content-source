Andrew had the aisle seat. His knees held the tray table shut in front of him, and his right shoulder occupied a portion of the seat beside his that the man assigned to it had quietly conceded. That man had assessed the situation during boarding, angled himself toward the window, and gone to sleep in self-defence.

Being the size he was had its advantages. Economy class was not one of them.

The coffee cart came through and caught his arm, and acquired a small dent in the process. The attendant looked at the dent, handed Andrew his coffee, and smiled at him the way people smile when they're not sure whose fault something is.

His bag was in the overhead. Inside it, rolled in a shirt, wrapped in a café rag from Naples, the bottle. It had gone through the scanner at Capodichino without comment. He had thought about that for a long time afterward.

The captain made an announcement that named the airport and the weather on the ground. Andrew put the phone away. The plane banked, and grey-green water came up under the wing, worked over by wind, and then land.

---

He saw Mike before the arrivals doors had fully parted. The crowd around him was making small adjustments, the way crowds do around large men.

The hug took organising. Two big men, a first attempt that put both right arms in the same place, a correction, Mike's hand landing twice on Andrew's back, hard. They got it settled and held it a second longer than logistics required.

When they stepped apart, Andrew looked at his face.

The beard was kept. The rest had been spent. The eyes sat deeper than they had in the selfie, the skin beneath them gone the colour of weak tea, and over the whole of him lay a stillness with nothing restful in it, the stillness of a man who had been holding a position for a long time. Andrew took all of it in and said it was good to see him.

"You too." Mike reached for the bag. Andrew kept it.

The car was in the short-stay, under a concrete pillar. A small grey hatchback, the kind of car a man buys at twenty-five because he needs one, with a physical key and a parking permit from a different life curling on the windscreen. Mike opened the passenger door first and lifted a stack of unopened post off the seat, wedging it down behind the handbrake.

Andrew reached for the seat adjuster. The seat was already as far back as the rail went. He folded himself in. His knees found the glovebox and his hair found the ceiling, and when Mike got in on the other side his shoulder arrived in the middle of the car. The economy seat had been practice.

"You still have this car."

"It does the shops."

Mike started the engine. They wound up through the short-stay levels, Mike taking the ramps at the speed of a man who had driven them many times, paid at the barrier, and came out into grey afternoon light.

When they found the airport road Andrew said: "How are you holding up?"

Mike looked at the road. "I'm functioning."

"How long has it been?"

"Long enough that I've stopped counting weeks." He changed lanes. "Long enough that I know his route."

Andrew nodded and left it there.

They found the city from the north, and Andrew knew it before the signs did. A retail park he had passed on a bus. The long street that had taken him to campus twice a week for eight months, the same launderette on its corner with the same dead plant in the window.

Where the road ran high ground for half a kilometre, the harbour opened out on the left. The Medved Bridge stood across its mouth, towers and cables against the afternoon, exactly where he had left it. A container ship was passing under it without ceremony. West of the bridge the docklands lay flat against the water, low roofs and gantries, and somewhere in among them a building he had once taken a bus to. Andrew gave that side of the city the time it took to check a mirror and put his eyes back on the road.

The last time he had crossed these streets at ground level he had weighed seventy-eight kilos and apologised to furniture. The city had not changed. The change was riding in the passenger seat with its knees against the glovebox.

"I found something in Naples. You'll want to see it."

Mike nodded at the road. "I've got things too."

---

The guest room was the guest room. Same bed, same small mirror on the wall by the door. Andrew had watched himself get smaller in that mirror once, day by day, more of the student arriving every morning, and the room held the memory without making anything of it. He put the bag on the bed and went back out.

The kitchen was clean in the thorough way of a man who needed his hands occupied. A grey hoodie hung over the back of one chair. Mike filled the kettle, set it on its base, and did not switch it on. He stood with one hand on the counter, looking at the window over the sink, and whatever he was looking at was not in the yard.

"Mike."

Mike turned. His hand came off the counter and he sat down across the table.

"There's a park on King Street," he said.

Andrew sat.

"Bill has him. You know that much." Mike turned his mug a half-turn on the table and left it. "I found the park. He uses it. I'd been on a bench past the fountain for three nights, where the lamps leave dark between them, far enough that a man on a bench is just a man on a bench."

He told it without performing it. He looked at the table for most of it and at the window for the rest.

"A man came up the path. Late twenties, soft build. Nervous in a way that hurt to watch, because you could see he'd talked himself into something and was hoping it would be kind to him." Mike stopped there for a moment. "Nick was at a tree past the next lamp, waiting for him. He's bigger than he was. Black on him from the chest down, the high-shine black, and his hair's long now. His eyes are violet." Mike said the colour and moved past it. "He talked to the man first. Quiet, warm. Patient. He asked the man what he wanted, and the man told him the truth, and then Nick kissed him, and whatever Bill runs through him went across in it. I heard the man's clothes give. I heard his voice change in the middle of a word. The whole time it was happening, Nick talked him through it. Gentle. He called him a good boy."

The kettle sat unboiled on its base. Mike's thumb found the table edge and stopped there.

"Twenty minutes after that man walked up the path, a different man walked into the trees behind Nick. Before they went, Nick turned and looked at the bench. At me. He held it long past what a man scanning a path would hold. He'd known I was there since before the other one arrived, and he let me stay for all of it." Mike looked up for a moment and then back down. "Nothing in that look was him. I sat on that bench afterward and I couldn't find one thing I'd watched that backed up what I came there believing."

He didn't say what that had cost him. The kitchen said it.

"You're the first person I've told." A beat. "Any of it."

Andrew held his eyes, kept his face flat, and bounced his chest once under his shirt. The fabric jumped. A seam near the shoulder made a short remark.

For about two seconds Mike's face had no weight on it. Then it did again.

Andrew put his hand on Mike's arm and left it there.

"The day you broke the harness," he said. "You remember the fight before it."

"I remember."

"I was in it. Underneath. The whole fight I watched my own arms from somewhere at the back of myself, and I couldn't reach them. I knew your cape. I knew what I was swinging at. None of that knowing moved a finger." He kept his voice even because the words did not need help. "What Bill puts into a man doesn't empty him out. It takes the wheel. Nick is in there watching every one of those men walk up that path, and none of what he's watching is moving his hands."

Mike sat with that. He didn't reach for the mug.

"Pieces," he said.

"Pieces. I got your cape. I got your crest. They surfaced and went under again, and they were mine the whole time." Andrew took the hand back. "Nobody was sitting where you're sitting telling me this, back then. You had to take it on faith. You don't have to now."

Mike looked at him for a long moment, and the set of his shoulders gave by a degree. The first degree it had given since the airport.

---

Andrew went to the guest room and came back with the rolled shirt. He unrolled it on the table, took the rag away, and set the bottle between them.

Dark glass, smaller than a beer bottle, a crimped metal cap. It was heavier than it looked, and the weight moved when it moved, slow, the contents finding their level a beat behind the glass. Holding it, his palm knew it. He set it down and took the hand back.

"My regular café in Naples. Brotherhood deliveries through the back room for months, and I drank espresso twenty feet from it every morning and read my book. There were three of them when I went in. Crates against the walls. Half the crates were these."

Mike was looking at the bottle.

"The owner's nephew works the deliveries. They went to the family in February. Threats, the usual shape of it. He kept the back room available and asked nothing. A week ago two of them argued about a shipment where he could hear, and one said three crates were for Marseille and the rest were going further. Then they noticed him and stopped talking." Andrew turned the bottle a quarter with one finger and took the finger back. "Bill stood over me to do what he did. He had to be in the room. These mean he doesn't have to be in the room."

"Is it what he used on you?"

"A version. Mine was smoke. This travels."

Mike kept his hands on his side of the table. The bottle sat there with the lamp finding a dull line along its shoulder, sealed, the cap unbroken, and neither of them moved it again.

Mike got up, took a sheet of paper folded in quarters from the drawer under the toaster, and opened it flat on the table beside the bottle.

"I've been on him for weeks. On foot, no suit. He moves on a pattern and most of it I can't use. But there's a unit in Harbour End he keeps going back to. Two streets off the water, with a shuttered front and deliveries after dark. Four visits that I've seen." He smoothed the fold out of the paper with the heel of his hand. "These went up on the hoarding outside it this week. There's a stack of them behind the glass too."

The poster was black. BLACK URSA across the top in white block letters, an address on the harbour side of the docklands beneath it, and a date.

"The name was in Nick's notes," Mike said.

Andrew looked at the poster, then at the bottle beside it, then at the poster.

"When."

"Saturday."

"Warehouse?"

"Warehouse."

Andrew turned the poster to face him. He read the address twice, the slow second read of a man who intended to stand on it.

"I didn't pack for a concert."

"We'll find you one."

Mike took the kettle off its base and filled it again, and this time he switched it on. Andrew pulled the poster the rest of the way across the table and started on the small print.
