## Superbear Chapter 12:

# The Scent of the First Day

Nick's glasses sat on the nightstand, folded neatly, the way Nick left everything.

Mike had been moving around them for five weeks. He hadn't touched them. He hadn't moved them. He'd cleaned around them, dusted the surface beside them, set his own phone and keys on the opposite end of the nightstand so nothing would disturb them. The lenses were clean. Nick hadn't needed glasses since the transformation — his eyes had sharpened along with everything else — but he'd kept wearing them sometimes around the apartment because he liked how they looked. Because Mike liked how they looked.

Five weeks. Thirty-five days of the same apartment, the same silence, the same box of Nick's investigation notes spread across the kitchen table.

Mike sat in front of them now. The overhead light hummed. The coffee in his mug had gone cold two hours ago. He'd read every page so many times the paper had softened at the edges from handling.

He picked up a page — Nick's handwriting, precise and slanted, mapping the Brotherhood's supply routes through the warehouse district. Cross-references in the margins. Annotations layered over annotations. Nick had built this picture over weeks, alone, because Mike had stopped listening.

That thought found him every morning. It waited for him at the kitchen table the way Nick's notes did.

He put the page down and pressed both hands flat against the table, feeling the wood grain against his palms. His breath was steady but his chest hurt. Everything hurt, a slow-burning ache behind his ribs that never fully settled and never sharpened enough to act on. He was stronger than anyone in this city and the strength meant nothing. He could lift a building, stop a bus, fly faster than any helicopter, and none of it could find Nick.

He'd tried everything.

Super-smell: the trail was five weeks cold. What traces remained dissolved into the underground somewhere in the industrial quarter, swallowed by concrete and depth. He'd circled the area for days, flying low, trying to catch anything residual. The city's noise — its exhaust, its sewage, its million daily cooking fires — buried whatever had been there.

Aerial surveillance: he'd mapped the industrial district from the air, memorised every building, every rooftop, every loading dock. He looked for patterns, for movement that didn't match the neighbourhood. He found nothing. The Brotherhood knew how to disappear.

Retracing Nick's steps: he'd walked them twice. From the apartment to the public library where Nick did his research, to the coffee shop where he'd sit and work, to the building Nick had identified in his notes as the Brotherhood facility where Evan was being held.

That building.

Mike had stood in front of it at three in the morning, alone. The loading dock shutter was rusted halfway down, exactly as Nick had described. Graffiti thick enough to blur into texture. The kind of place the city had stopped seeing.

Inside, he found nothing.

The building had been emptied. Swept clean. No cots, no chairs, no straps on shelves. Even the chemical traces were gone — not masked, but scoured. Someone had gone through the entire facility with the kind of thoroughness that took planning. They hadn't left in a hurry. They'd had time.

They'd known Nick was coming.

Mike stood in the empty corridor where his partner had fought four men and felt the air close around him. The concrete was new. The pipes along the ceiling were intact. No cracks, no dents, no sign that anything had happened here at all.

They'd repaired it. All of it. Every trace of Nick erased.

After that there was nothing left to try.

The bear energy connection — the thing Mike had always felt humming faintly between them, even at a distance, even when they were in separate parts of the city — was gone. Silent. He'd reached for it every day, pressing inward with everything he had, searching for even the thinnest thread of Nick's frequency. He found nothing. The space where Nick used to be felt sealed over. Closed. As if the connection had been cut at the root.

That terrified him more than everything else combined.

Because if Nick's bear energy was gone — if whatever they'd done to him had reached deep enough to change the fundamental frequency of who he was — then the Nick who came back might not be Nick at all.

Mike pushed back from the table and stood. His body felt heavy. He hadn't been on patrol in weeks. The city needed Superbear and Superbear wasn't there. Part of him registered the guilt of that — people counting on him, people in trouble, the suit hanging in the closet unworn — and the rest of him couldn't make himself care enough to act on it. Not with Nick missing. Not with this.

He moved through the apartment the way he'd been moving through it for five weeks: carefully, around the edges, avoiding the places that felt most like Nick. The couch where they watched movies. The kitchen counter where Nick leaned while Mike cooked. The bedroom door, which Mike kept half-closed because the empty bed was worse than the empty kitchen.

He stopped at the kitchen table and looked at the box of notes again.

There was nothing new in them. He'd memorised every page. He'd followed every lead. He'd gone to every location, checked every cross-reference, verified every annotation. Nick's investigation had been ready. The intelligence was solid. The plan was sound.

Mike had shut it down. Every time Nick brought something new, Mike had found a reason to wait. Too dangerous. Too early. Not enough information.

And Nick went alone.

Mike picked up the box. Heavy cardboard, the kind that held printer paper. Nick had labelled it in black marker on the side: BROTHERHOOD — WORKING. The handwriting was measured and precise, the same careful lettering Nick used for everything.

He tilted the box to sort through the bottom layer — pages he'd already read, early drafts, discarded leads. As he shifted them, something slipped from the fold of the cardboard itself. A small rectangle of paper, wedged between the inner wall and the bottom flap, invisible unless you tilted the box at exactly this angle.

Mike set the box down and picked up the paper.

His breath stopped.

The ink was slightly smudged, as if written with care rather than speed. The handwriting was unfamiliar — looping, generous, written by someone who took their time.

> *Dear Mike,*
>
> *if you believe in yourself, you can be super!*
>
> *Love, Daddy Bear.*

The note. The original note. From the box of Power Bear Coffee.

Mike held it in both hands and stared at it. He hadn't thought about this note in — how long? Since the first day. Since before any of this. He'd found it on his doorstep with a box of coffee he didn't understand, brewed a cup out of curiosity, and everything that followed had swallowed the note entirely. He'd been busy becoming Superbear. Busy learning to fly. Busy falling in love with Nick. The note had been a curiosity, a cheesy motivational card from someone he'd never met, and he'd set it aside the way you set aside a fortune cookie fortune — charmed, briefly, and then forgotten.

Nick had found it.

Mike turned the note over. Nick's handwriting covered the back, smaller and more urgent than his usual script.

*Who is "Daddy Bear"? Note arrived same day as the coffee — clearly connected. Handwriting doesn't match any known associates. Paper is ordinary — card stock, no watermark, no print shop markings. Ink is standard ballpoint.*

*Distinctive smell on the paper — faint, hard to place. Tried tracing it 3x, super-smell still too underdeveloped. Can't isolate the scent from the ambient paper smell. Filed as UNRESOLVED.*

*Whoever sent this knew Mike's name and address. They knew about the coffee. They may know about the transformation. If there's a person behind all of this — behind Superbear — this note is the only direct connection to them.*

Mike read the annotations twice. Then he brought the note to his nose.

His super-smell was fully developed. He'd had months — years — of practice. He could separate a scent into its components the way a musician separates notes in a chord.

The note carried paper, ink, age, the faint residual oils from Nick's fingers. And beneath all of that, something else. Something specific and layered — woodsmoke, pine resin, a dense earthiness that reminded Mike of old growth forest. Underneath it, something warmer and harder to name: the faintest trace of something that felt like bear energy itself, condensed and fading but unmistakable.

A person. A real person, somewhere in the world, with this scent.

The person who had started everything.

Mike stood in his kitchen holding the note and felt something shift behind his ribs. He felt stupid for not thinking of this sooner. He'd been a superhero for — how long now — and he'd never once gone back to the beginning. Never asked who sent the coffee. Never wondered why a stranger would place that kind of power on his doorstep and walk away. He'd been so busy being Superbear that he'd forgotten Superbear had a cause.

Nick had found it. Nick, with his investigator's instinct, had picked up the note, smelled something Mike had missed entirely, and filed it as unresolved because he didn't yet have the tools to trace it.

Mike did.

He brought the note to his nose again. The scent was faint but specific — a signature, layered deep into the fibres, old enough to have faded but stubborn enough to remain. He breathed it in and held it, letting his super-smell build a map the way it always did: direction, distance, the way a scent thins and concentrates along a path.

Northwest. Outside the city. Past the industrial edge, past the suburbs, into open land where the air thinned and the trees thickened.

For the first time in five weeks, Mike had a direction.

---

He flew in the suit.

The city dropped away below him and he didn't look back. The suit felt stiff — he hadn't worn it in weeks, and his body had to remember the way it moved, the way the cape caught the wind, the way the bear paw emblem sat against his chest. He flew high and fast, above the cloud layer where no one could see him, following the scent the way a compass follows north.

The city gave way to suburbs. The suburbs gave way to fields. The fields gave way to forest — dense, old, untouched. He dropped below the treeline and slowed, letting the scent guide him through a landscape that felt deliberately removed from everything. No roads. No houses. No sound except wind through pine needles and the distant knock of a woodpecker.

The lake appeared first. Small, still, reflecting the sky like a sheet of grey glass. Then the cabin.

It was simple. Log walls, a stone chimney, a porch with two wooden chairs and a splitting block. Firewood stacked neatly against the east wall. Smoke rising from the chimney in a thin line. The kind of place that looked like it had been here longer than anything around it, grown from the landscape rather than built on it.

Mike landed at the edge of the tree line, fifty yards out. He stood for a moment in the suit, cape settling against his back, looking at the cabin. Then he closed his eyes and de-transformed. The suit dissolved. The cape vanished. The emblem faded. When he opened his eyes he was Mike again — jeans, a grey hoodie, sneakers. Ordinary. Smaller.

He walked the rest of the way on foot.

The man was chopping wood.

He was old. That was the first thing Mike registered — old in a way that went deeper than age, as if something had been drawn out of him a long time ago and the body had kept going without it. His frame was large — broad through the shoulders, heavy through the chest — but the muscle had wasted. The arms that swung the axe did it with practice rather than power, each stroke placed precisely because strength could no longer compensate for error. His beard was patchy and grey. His hair was thin under a worn wool hat. His clothes were layered and functional — flannel, canvas, work boots — the kind of clothing chosen to keep warm, not to be seen.

He looked like someone the world had already decided didn't matter.

But his scent matched the note exactly. Every layer of it — the woodsmoke, the pine, the deep earthy warmth, the faint residual trace of something that had once been immense. This was the man. This was the source.

The old man set the axe into the splitting block and straightened. He looked at Mike across the clearing with eyes that were heavy with memory but entirely present. He didn't seem surprised.

He seemed like he'd been waiting.

Mike stopped at the edge of the porch. His throat was tight. He looked at the man who had started everything and tried to find the right words. Something adequate. Something that matched the weight of the moment.

What came out was quieter than he expected.

"I've been trying to believe in myself," Mike said. "I don't feel very super right now."

The old man's eyes moved across Mike's face. Reading him. Taking in the exhaustion, the grief, the five weeks of searching that sat in the hollows beneath Mike's eyes and the set of his jaw.

"You found the note," the old man said. His voice was low and unhurried, carrying the same quality as the cabin itself — something built to last, worn by time, still standing.

"Nick found it first," Mike said. "He noticed the smell. He couldn't trace it. I could."

A pause. The old man nodded slowly, as if confirming something to himself.

"Then you'd better come sit down," he said. "I'll make coffee. Regular, this time."

---

He led Mike around the side of the cabin to a pair of weathered Adirondack chairs facing the lake. The view was wide and still — water, trees, sky, nothing else. The distance from the city was physical and total. Out here the air smelled like pine and cold stone and the kind of quiet that made Mike's chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with grief.

The old man went inside. Mike heard water running, the clink of a kettle on a stove. He sat in the chair and stared at the lake and breathed.

When the old man came back with two mugs, Mike took his without speaking. The coffee was black, strong, and entirely ordinary. He wrapped both hands around the mug and held it the way he'd held a thousand mugs before, feeling the warmth seep into his fingers.

The old man sat in the other chair and sipped his own coffee and waited.

Everything Mike had been carrying for five weeks rose at once.

"I let him go alone." His voice cracked on the third word. "He told me — he showed me everything he'd found, and I kept shutting him down. Every time. I was trying to protect him and all I did was push him away until he stopped telling me things, and then he went alone, and they—"

He stopped. His jaw worked. His hands shook around the mug.

"They took him. They took him and I can't find him. I've tried everything. I went to the building — they cleared it out. The whole place, cleaned, repaired, like he was never there. I can't smell him. I can't feel him. The connection we had — the bear energy — it's gone. I reach for it and there's nothing. Five weeks of nothing."

His voice broke entirely. He pressed one hand against his face and his shoulders folded inward. The sound that came out of him was ugly and raw and he didn't try to contain it. He hadn't cried in front of anyone for five weeks because there was no one to cry in front of. He hadn't been able to tell anyone what had happened, what he'd done, what he'd failed to do. Doing so would have meant explaining Nick, explaining Supercub, explaining everything. The isolation had compounded every day until the grief and the guilt had fused into something he carried in his chest like a second heartbeat.

Here, in front of this man he'd never met, the dam broke.

The old man set his coffee down. He stood, crossed the small distance between the chairs, and pulled Mike into a hug.

It wasn't gentle. It was encompassing — arms around Mike's shoulders, one hand on the back of his head, pulling him in with a firmness that expected nothing in return. Mike's face pressed against the old man's chest and he could smell the flannel and the woodsmoke and the deep, residual warmth of something that had once been vast. The frame was frail but the hold was sure.

Mike sobbed. His whole body shook with it. Five weeks of silence and searching and moving carefully around Nick's glasses on the nightstand came out of him in a sound he barely recognised as his own. He gripped the old man's jacket and held on.

The old man held him. He didn't speak. He didn't soothe or reassure or try to manage what was happening. He stood solid and let Mike shake against him until the shaking ran its course.

When Mike pulled back, his eyes were red and his face was wet. He wiped it with his sleeve, roughly, embarrassed and relieved in equal measure.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't be." The old man returned to his chair, picked up his coffee, and sat. He looked at Mike the way you look at something you built a long time ago, seeing it for the first time as it actually turned out. There was grief in that look, and pride, and something Mike couldn't yet name.

"The note," Mike said. He cleared his throat. "You sent the coffee. The Power Bear Coffee. You sent it to me."

"I did."

"The note was — I mean—" Mike almost smiled, despite everything. "It was pretty cheesy."

The old man's expression shifted. The faintest crack of amusement in the heavy lines of his face. "I'm not much of a writer."

"But the scent was deliberate."

"The scent was the whole point." He held his mug in both hands, the same way Mike did. "The words were cover. Something a person would read and forget. The scent was a way back to me, if you ever needed one. Built in. Permanent."

"A failsafe."

"Something like that."

Mike looked at him — really looked, past the frailty and the age and the way the world had shrunk him down to something unremarkable. The frame was still there beneath the wasting: the broad shoulders, the heavy chest, the stance of someone who had once occupied space the way a mountain occupies a valley. He could see what this man had been, the way you could see a cathedral in a ruin.

"You're the one who made the coffee," Mike said. "The dust. The rainbow dust — you put it in the coffee."

"Yes."

"And you — you used to be—" Mike stopped. The old man didn't fill the silence. He let Mike arrive at it himself.

Through the conversation, through the way the old man spoke about bear energy with a specificity that couldn't be secondhand, through the scent that matched the note that had arrived with the coffee that had contained the last remaining fragment of a power that once held transformation itself in balance — the picture assembled.

"You were the guardian," Mike said. "Before Bill. Before any of this. You held the power. You were — Daddy Bear."

The old man looked at the lake. The name sat between them like something he hadn't heard spoken aloud in a very long time.

"I was," he said.

Mike's coffee had gone cold in his hands. He set it down on the arm of the chair.

"What happened to you?"

"Bill happened." The old man said it without bitterness. A fact. A consequence. "I opened a threshold for him. An invitation. He didn't accept it — he took what was behind it. Everything I carried, all the active power — he ripped it out of me. The system fractured. What came through the other side was him, transformed, but wrong. Corrupted. Power without consent."

"Butch Bear."

"Butch Bear." The old man nodded. "After that, I had almost nothing left. Enough to condense the residual potential into dust — the rainbow dust — and put it somewhere it could be found by someone who deserved it. Someone who would choose to drink a cup of coffee because they were curious, not because they were hungry for power."

"Me."

"You." The old man looked at him again with that expression Mike was starting to understand — the look of someone seeing the outcome of their last act of agency, alive and grieving in a chair beside them. "I watched you, after. From a distance. I saw you become Superbear. I saw you learn to fly, learn to help, learn to use what you'd been given. I stayed close long enough to know you were using the power the way it was meant to be used. Then I came out here."

"Why didn't you come to me sooner?" Mike's voice was steady now. The crying was done. What replaced it was something more functional — present, clear-eyed, asking the questions he needed answered. "When Nick was taken. You knew about Bill. You knew what he could do."

The old man was quiet for a moment. His thumb traced the rim of his mug.

"My role ended when I gave the power away. I don't intervene. I don't choose outcomes. I placed possibility into the world and stepped back. That was the last thing the system allowed me to do." He paused. "I assumed, if you ever truly needed me, you'd find your way here through the note. It was designed for that."

"It took me five weeks."

"It did."

"Was that—" Mike stopped, recalculated. "Were you right to wait?"

The old man didn't defend himself. "I don't know," he said. "I believed you'd come. Whether I should have done more than believe — that's a question I'll sit with."

The honesty of it surprised Mike. He'd expected a justification, a principle, a reason. Instead he got a man who wasn't sure he'd made the right call and was willing to say so.

Mike leaned forward in his chair. "Tell me about Nick. Tell me what Bill did to him."

The old man held up one hand, gently. "I need to be honest with you. I don't know Nick. I never met him. I gave away almost all of my power — I can't see or sense what Bill has done. I don't have a window into that operation."

Mike's stomach dropped. "Then—"

"What I can do is reason from what I know. About bear energy. About Bill. About how corruption works." He set his mug down on the porch railing. "You said the bear energy connection between you and Nick is gone. That you reach for it and feel nothing."

"Nothing. Complete silence."

"That's consistent with what I'd expect if Bill corrupted him. Corrupted bearing changes the frequency of the energy. It doesn't destroy it — it overwrites it. Rewrites the signal into something that speaks Bill's language instead of yours. You can't feel Nick because the part of him that was tuned to you has been retuned to Bill."

Mike felt the words land in his chest like stones.

"But the original frequency doesn't disappear," the old man continued. "It gets buried. Layered over. The corruption is louder, but the foundation is still there, underneath. Bill's power is stolen power — it's strong, but it's structurally unstable. It overwrites, but it doesn't integrate. The person underneath is still present. They're just harder to reach."

"You think Nick is still in there."

"I think the person you knew is underneath whatever Bill has built on top of him. Whether he can reach the surface — whether you can reach him — depends on how deep the corruption went and how strong the original connection was."

"It was strong." Mike said it immediately. "The connection. What we had. It was—"

He stopped. He didn't need to explain it to a stranger. But the old man didn't look like a stranger. He looked like someone who understood what it cost to give power away and watch from a distance as it found its purpose.

"Then there's something to reach for," the old man said.

---

The light was changing. Late afternoon, the sun dropping behind the treeline, casting long shadows across the lake. The air had cooled. The old man had refilled their mugs once and they sat in the chairs and talked the way two people talk when they've both been carrying something too heavy to put down.

Mike had told him about the pulse.

The night Nick was taken — the night Mike had been lying in bed feeling the connection go silent — there had been a moment. Brief, faint, almost swallowed by the noise of his own panic. A pulse of bear energy, raw and undirected, reaching for him through layers of something dense and wrong. It had lasted less than a second. Mike had felt it the way you feel a hand brush your arm in a crowd — there and gone before you can turn.

He'd spent five weeks wondering if he'd imagined it.

"You didn't imagine it," the old man said. "That was him. That was Nick, reaching for you through the corruption before it finished sealing. A pulse like that — sent from inside a system designed to prevent exactly that kind of connection — that takes everything a person has. He spent his last free moment trying to tell you where he was."

Mike's eyes burned. He pressed the heels of his hands against them and breathed.

"Can I use that?" he asked. "The connection. Can I find him through it?"

The old man studied him. The heavy, perceptive eyes moved across Mike's face, assessing something Mike couldn't see.

"The bear energy that connects you to Nick is still there," the old man said. "The corruption has buried it, but it hasn't severed it. What you felt that night — that pulse — proves the link exists. But you've been reaching for it with grief. With panic. With five weeks of desperation clouding everything. Bear energy doesn't respond to force. It responds to clarity."

He leaned forward.

"Close your eyes."

Mike hesitated. Then he set his coffee down and closed his eyes.

"Your bear energy is the strongest thing about you," the old man said. His voice was steady and warm, the cadence of someone who had guided this kind of work a thousand times before he lost the ability to do it himself. "Stronger than your muscles. Stronger than your flight. You've been trying to find Nick by reaching outward — searching, grasping, throwing yourself at the silence. Stop reaching. Let it settle."

Mike breathed. His hands rested on the arms of the chair. He could hear the lake, the wind, the faint creak of the cabin behind them.

"Bear energy is a frequency," the old man continued. "Yours has a specific signature — warm, open, generous. Nick's has a different one — measured, steady, precise. You know his frequency the way you know his voice. You could pick it out of a crowd."

Mike breathed deeper. The panic was still there — a humming tension in his chest, the five weeks of frantic searching coiled tight behind his ribs. He let it be there without feeding it.

"Don't search for him. Let the frequency come to you. You already know what Nick feels like. Stop trying to find it and start listening for it."

The silence stretched. Mike sat with his eyes closed and his hands open and listened.

Nothing.

The wind moved through the pines. The lake lapped softly at the shore. His own heartbeat measured itself against the stillness.

Nothing.

He breathed. He let the grief sit where it was — heavy, present, undeniable — and stopped pushing it at the silence. He stopped reaching. He stopped searching. He sat still and listened the way you listen for a sound you've heard a thousand times, so familiar you could hallucinate it.

Then — faintly — something.

A warmth. Small, specific, buried deep beneath layers of something dark and heavy that pressed against it from all sides. It was faint the way a candle is faint at the end of a long hallway — barely there, easily mistaken for nothing. But Mike knew the quality of that warmth the way he knew the sound of Nick's laugh. Measured. Steady. Precise.

Nick.

Mike's breath caught. His hands gripped the arms of the chair. The warmth flickered, almost disappeared, then steadied — a tiny point of light holding itself together inside something vast and dark, refusing to go out.

"There," Mike whispered. "I feel him. He's — he's there. He's faint, but he's there."

"Hold it," the old man said. His voice was close and firm. "Don't chase it. Hold it. Let it sit in you the way his voice sits in your memory. Learn its direction."

Mike held the feeling. It was like cupping water in his hands — fragile, constantly threatening to slip away, demanding a stillness he wasn't sure he could sustain. But it was there. After five weeks of silence, it was there.

South. Underground. Distant, but specific. A direction.

Mike opened his eyes.

The lake was golden in the last light. The old man sat beside him, watching, his heavy eyes carrying something that looked like the first hope he'd felt in a very long time.

"I know where to go," Mike said.

He stood. His body felt different — the heaviness of the past five weeks hadn't lifted, but something had shifted beneath it. A thread, thin and fragile, connecting him to a point in the dark. A compass needle pointing south.

He looked at the old man — frail, diminished, sitting in a wooden chair by a lake with nothing left to give except the one thing he'd just given.

"Thank you," Mike said. The words felt small for what they carried, but they were the right ones.

The old man nodded. He didn't stand. He didn't offer anything else. He sat in his chair and looked at Mike the way a father looks at a son leaving for something the father can no longer follow him into.

"Go find him," he said.

Mike walked to the edge of the clearing. He closed his eyes, felt the thread of Nick's warmth holding steady in his chest, and transformed. The suit wrapped around him — blue and red and gold, the cape unfurling behind him, the bear paw emblem settling over his heart. Superbear stood where Mike had been, hands at his sides, facing south.

He launched into the sky. The cabin shrank below him, then the lake, then the trees. The old man in the chair grew small and still, watching the trail of rainbow energy arc across the darkening sky.

Then Mike was gone, and Daddy Bear was alone with the last of the light.
